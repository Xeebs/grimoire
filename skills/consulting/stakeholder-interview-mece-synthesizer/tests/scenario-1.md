# Scenario 1: Procurement Centralization — Mid-Market Manufacturing Client

## Context

A management consultant (second-year associate) at a mid-market strategy firm is preparing the synthesis package for a manufacturing client's procurement operating model review. The engagement team conducted 20 interviews over three weeks across five stakeholder groups. The synthesis workshop with the client's leadership team is in 48 hours. The associate has all interview notes and must produce a structured recommendation brief before the team's internal alignment session tomorrow morning.

The client — MidWest Fabrication Group, a $600M industrial components manufacturer — is weighing whether to consolidate procurement from five semi-autonomous regional operations into a centralized national function. The CFO initiated the project after benchmarking data showed MWF's procurement cost as a percentage of spend was 40 basis points above industry median. A centralization attempt in 2019 was abandoned after two Regional GMs threatened to escalate to the board, citing a breakdown in emergency procurement response during a plant retooling.

## Input

**CLIENT QUESTION**: Should we centralize procurement and if so, how and at what pace?

**ENGAGEMENT**: MidWest Fabrication Group — Procurement Operating Model Review
**INTERVIEW SCOPE**: 20 interviews | CFO (1), Regional General Managers (3), Procurement Leads (4), Plant Managers (5), Key External Suppliers (2), Operations Directors (2), Finance Business Partners (2), HR Lead (1)

---

### INTERVIEW — CFO, Executive Leadership, HQ Chicago

The 40bp gap versus benchmark is not sustainable. We spend $180M annually on direct and indirect materials. If centralization closes even half the gap, that is $12–15M to the bottom line before any process efficiency gains. The benchmark study shows our top 12 suppliers are each contracted with at least three different regional entities at different price points — that is pure volume leverage left on the table. I have the board's support to move on this. I need an implementation plan that does not repeat 2019. The 2019 failure was a people problem, not a structural problem — we promoted the wrong person to lead the central function and they had no operational credibility with the plants. We also did not fund the change management properly. I am not letting cost-saving inertia from the regions kill a $15M annual opportunity again.

---

### INTERVIEW — Regional GM, Great Lakes Region

I am not opposed to coordination on large strategic categories. Stainless steel, copper wire — yes, let's negotiate those nationally. But if you take procurement authority away from my team for MRO and local services, we will be ordering safety gloves through a six-week approval queue from someone in Chicago who has never seen a plant floor. I have $4M in annual emergency procurement — parts failures, equipment downtime events. My team can get a replacement part in 48 hours because they have relationships with three local distributors who will take a call on Sunday night. A central team cannot do that. The 2019 pilot proved it. We had a 17-day wait for a conveyor belt part because it wasn't on the approved vendor list. I will take this to the board if the proposal doesn't protect emergency procurement autonomy.

---

### INTERVIEW — Regional GM, Southeast Region

I am more open to this than my Great Lakes colleague. My concern is category expertise. If you build a central team, you need people who understand the category nuances of each region's spend profile. Southeast spend is heavily weighted to aluminum extrusion — it's a regional commodity market and the pricing dynamics are completely different from what's happening in the Midwest. A generalist procurement team in Chicago will get us worse contracts, not better. But if you staff the central function with people who have real category depth, I can see the value. My other concern is the transition. My procurement lead has 11 years with MWF — if she does not have a role in the new structure, I am going to lose her, and her institutional knowledge walks out the door.

---

### INTERVIEW — Regional GM, Southwest Region

I will tell you what I told the team in 2019: the savings estimates always look good on paper and they never survive contact with reality. The benchmark data assumes we are buying the same things at scale, but our product mix is more customized than the benchmark peers. Our suppliers have to be flexible on specs and lead times — that is worth money that does not show up in unit price comparisons. I have been at MWF for 22 years. I have seen three centralization attempts. None of them delivered what was promised. The issue is not procurement structure, the issue is that HQ does not understand our regional customers. Fix the demand forecasting, not the procurement org.

---

### INTERVIEW — Procurement Lead, Great Lakes Region

If centralization is done properly, I think it can work. The problem in 2019 was that the central team had authority without accountability — they controlled the purchase orders but didn't own the plant uptime metrics. If you centralize, you need to tie the central team's performance metrics to the same plant availability KPIs the plant managers care about. Otherwise you get the same behavior as 2019: optimize for unit price, ignore lead time risk. I would also flag that we currently have no common ERP platform across regions. Great Lakes is on SAP, Southeast is on Oracle, Southwest is on a legacy system. You cannot centralize effectively without solving the data problem first, and that is a $3–5M project on its own.

---

### INTERVIEW — Procurement Lead, Southeast Region

We spend approximately $8M annually with regional suppliers who would not survive a centralized volume consolidation model. Three of those suppliers provide same-day delivery for specialty items — they compete on service, not price, and a national RFP process would eliminate them. I am not saying we shouldn't centralize, but the savings model needs to account for the service premium we would lose. Also: our regional supplier relationships have created goodwill that helps us during supply crunches. In 2021, when everyone was fighting for aluminum, our regional contacts prioritized our orders because of the relationship. A central team in Chicago does not have that.

---

### INTERVIEW — Procurement Lead, Southwest Region

I have been doing this job for 8 years. A central team has never solved our biggest problem, which is demand volatility. Our plant schedules change on two weeks' notice and we buy to match. The contracts that look good on paper have minimum volume commitments that become a liability when a customer delays a project. If you lock us into consolidated contracts, you lock us into minimum purchase obligations that cost us more when demand drops than we saved on unit price. I have three examples from 2020 I can share. This needs to be modeled, not assumed away.

---

### INTERVIEW — Procurement Lead, HQ Chicago (Category Manager, Metals)

I support centralization. I am the only person in MWF with a view across all five regions' metals spend and the picture is embarrassing. We have 14 active contracts for stainless steel across the business. Three of those contracts are with the same supplier at three different price points — the highest price is 22% above the lowest. Volume leverage on metals alone would deliver $4–6M in year-one savings. The structural argument for centralization is airtight for direct materials. Indirect spend is more complicated and I would not rush it.

---

### INTERVIEW — Plant Manager, Great Lakes Plant A

My primary concern is emergency response time. We run on a 72-hour parts window — if a line goes down and we cannot source a part within 72 hours, we miss customer commitments and face contractual penalties. Under the current model, my procurement team has standing authorization to place emergency orders up to $50K without additional approval. They do this on average 8 times a month. If centralization adds a 24-hour approval loop, we will miss our SLA windows.

---

### INTERVIEW — Plant Manager, Great Lakes Plant B

I think centralization could actually help us. I spend too much time on procurement issues that should be handled by professionals with category expertise. My procurement support right now is one coordinator who is overwhelmed. A proper central team that handles the strategic procurement decisions would free my team to focus on operations. My only ask is that we preserve local discretion for MRO below some dollar threshold — say, $25K.

---

### INTERVIEW — Plant Manager, Southeast Plant A

My concern is the transition period. If we are running two systems in parallel during the cutover, we will have purchasing chaos. The 2019 attempt had a six-month transition period where nobody knew whether to call the regional team or the central team. We lost two key suppliers during that period because they didn't know who to send invoices to. The transition plan needs to be more detailed than anything I have seen from corporate.

---

### INTERVIEW — Plant Manager, Southwest Plant A

I do not have strong views on the structure. Whatever lets me get parts faster and cheaper is fine with me. My only concern is category expertise — the person managing my spend needs to understand industrial automation components. This is not a commodity category.

---

### INTERVIEW — Plant Manager, Southwest Plant B

I would like to see the savings shared with the plants, not absorbed at the corporate level. If my plant is giving up procurement autonomy, I want the financial benefit reflected in my plant's P&L, not captured at HQ. Otherwise I have no incentive to cooperate with the transition.

---

### INTERVIEW — Key External Supplier A (Regional Distributor, Great Lakes)

Honestly, I prefer the current model. My relationship is with the Great Lakes team — I have been working with their procurement lead for six years. If purchasing goes to Chicago, my account becomes one of dozens. I will be competing against national distributors on price and I will lose. I provide value through service and flexibility, not unit price. A centralized RFP would eliminate us.

---

### INTERVIEW — Key External Supplier B (National Distributor, HQ relationship)

Centralization would benefit us significantly. Right now, we have contracts with three of the five regions but our pricing is inconsistent because each region negotiated independently. A consolidated contract with volume commitments would let us offer MWF our best pricing tier — we estimate a 12–15% reduction from current regional contract pricing on our categories. We would also assign a dedicated account manager, which we currently cannot justify given the fragmented relationship.

---

### INTERVIEW — Operations Director, Great Lakes

The ERP fragmentation is the issue nobody wants to talk about. You cannot run a central procurement function without a common data platform. Category managers need real-time visibility into plant-level demand and inventory. Right now, the only way to get that data is to call the regional teams and ask them to pull it manually. The ERP consolidation has to come before or alongside any procurement centralization. I have been saying this for three years.

---

### INTERVIEW — Operations Director, Southwest

I am aligned with centralization for strategic categories. My worry is that the change management budget will be cut before the transformation is complete. This happened with the last two centralization programs. HQ announces the initiative, funds year one, and then redirects the budget when something else takes priority. The regions have learned to wait it out. If you want this to stick, you need a multi-year budget commitment, not an annual discretionary line.

---

### INTERVIEW — Finance Business Partner, Great Lakes Region

The 2019 savings estimates assumed 100% of the volume leverage would be captured in year one. The actual capture rate was 35% because regional teams continued to place orders outside the consolidated contracts. Contract compliance is the real risk. You can centralize the purchasing function, but if plants bypass the system for emergency orders, you don't capture the savings. The compliance enforcement mechanism has to be designed before launch, not after.

---

### INTERVIEW — Finance Business Partner, HQ

My analysis shows the $12–15M savings estimate is achievable but back-loaded. Year-one savings would be $3–4M as contracts are renegotiated and the new function is staffed. Year two would be $7–9M as compliance improves. Full run-rate in year three. The CFO's expectation of full-year savings in year one is not realistic and I am concerned the board presentation will set an expectation the business cannot meet.

---

### INTERVIEW — HR Lead, HQ

The talent question is critical and underweighted. A central procurement function needs 8–10 FTEs with category expertise and ERP proficiency. We do not have those people internally — our regional procurement coordinators are generalists, not category managers. External hiring for those roles is running 6–8 months at minimum in this market. We also need to address what happens to the regional procurement staff whose roles are eliminated or significantly changed. We have approximately 22 people across the regions in procurement-adjacent roles. Without a clear TUPE/transition policy, we will face attrition of the people we most want to retain.

---

## Expected Output Criteria

- [ ] The Decision Frame (Step 1) correctly identifies the binary choice (centralize vs. regional autonomy), the constraints (2019 failure, board support, cost target), and the time horizon — and flags that the client question has two parts: whether to centralize AND how/at what pace
- [ ] The MECE issue tree contains at least 4 distinct top-level buckets and at least 2 sub-themes per bucket; no single claim from the interview notes is left unassigned
- [ ] The mutual exclusivity test is applied explicitly: the output states at least one boundary resolution where a claim was assigned to one bucket rather than another and explains why
- [ ] The Contradiction Register contains at least 3 contradictions: (1) CFO's savings estimate vs. Finance BP's year-one capture rate disagreement, (2) Regional GMs' agility claim (emergency response will break) vs. the plant manager who believes centralization would actually help, and (3) Regional supplier preference for the current model vs. National supplier's endorsement of centralization — each with nature classification and resolution path
- [ ] The Southwest GM's challenge to the savings methodology (demand volatility / minimum volume commitments) is not treated as a preference contradiction but is flagged as a data gap requiring ERP spend analysis
- [ ] Every finding uses "We believe X because Y" framing with a specific stakeholder group cited as evidence — no finding is a neutral summary
- [ ] The ERP fragmentation issue appears as either a Governance finding or as a pre-condition for centralization, with a recommendation labeled Quick Win or as a prerequisite step — it must not be absent from the output
- [ ] The recommendation brief includes a phased approach (direct materials first, indirect/MRO later) as at least one Strategic Bet recommendation, reflecting the convergent view across Procurement Leads and the HQ category manager
- [ ] The Great Lakes GM's explicit board escalation threat is noted — either in the Contradiction Register's resolution path, the Open Questions section, or as a feasibility constraint on the relevant recommendation
- [ ] The 2019 failure is referenced in at least one finding or recommendation, treated as evidence rather than as background noise — specifically, the contract compliance failure mechanism (regional bypass of consolidated contracts) must appear
- [ ] The Executive Summary contains exactly three key messages, each a single declarative sentence, and collectively they answer the client question (whether to centralize, the main risk/condition, and the action implication)
- [ ] At least one item appears in Peripheral Findings / Monitoring Items (the plant manager's request that savings be shared with plant P&Ls is a reasonable candidate — important but not a structural decision for the operating model)

## What failure looks like

A failing output would: produce a flat theme list instead of a nested MECE issue tree; write findings as "stakeholders had mixed views on centralization" without committing to a directional conclusion; miss the CFO vs. Finance BP contradiction on year-one savings capture (this is the most analytically important contradiction in the data — it means the board will be given an unrealistic expectation); omit the ERP fragmentation issue entirely (several interviewees raised it as a prerequisite condition and it has a $3–5M cost implication); assign all five GMs to the same stakeholder group when in fact their views diverge significantly (the Great Lakes GM is explicitly threatening escalation; the Southeast GM is open to centralization with conditions; the Southwest GM is a structural skeptic — lumping them as "Regional GMs oppose centralization" is analytically false and dangerous for the client); or produce recommendations without impact/feasibility ratings, leaving the client with no prioritization guidance.
