# Scenario 2: New AI-Enabled Service Line Viability — Professional Services Firm

## Context

A management consultant (third-year analyst, approaching promotion to associate) at a mid-size professional services firm is preparing a go/no-go recommendation for the firm's own strategic planning committee. The engagement is internal: the firm is evaluating whether to launch an AI-enabled advisory service line targeting mid-market CFOs. The firm's managing partner committee initiated the analysis after two competitors publicly announced AI-native offerings. The consultant conducted 15 interviews over two weeks. The strategy committee meeting is in three days. The analyst must produce a synthesized recommendation brief that will determine whether the firm commits an estimated $3.5M in Year 1 investment.

The firm — Vantage Advisory Partners, a 120-person professional services firm specializing in finance transformation and technology advisory — currently has no dedicated AI practice. Their AI capability exists informally in isolated pockets: three senior associates who have self-taught AI tooling and occasionally use it on client engagements without a formal offering structure. Two competitors (BCG X and a boutique called Luminary AI Advisory) have already launched structured AI advisory offerings targeting the same CFO persona.

## Input

**CLIENT QUESTION**: Is there a viable market for an AI-enabled advisory offering targeting mid-market CFOs, and what would it take for Vantage to win?

**ENGAGEMENT**: Vantage Advisory Partners — AI Service Line Feasibility Study
**INTERVIEW SCOPE**: 15 interviews | Managing Partners (3), Practice Leads (4), Senior Associates (4), Existing Clients / CFOs (4)

---

### INTERVIEW — Managing Partner A (Firm Leadership, Finance Transformation Practice)

This is existential for us. Our clients are starting to ask us directly: "what is your AI story?" We have no answer. BCG X has a story. Luminary has a story. We are a premium firm and we are being asked to justify our fees against tools that cost $500/month. We need to launch something — the question is what and how fast. My instinct is that we do not need to build AI capabilities from scratch. We should acquire or partner with a boutique AI firm and put our client relationships and domain expertise in front of their technology. I believe we can have something in market in 6 months if we move decisively. The market is ready — our clients are drowning in data and desperate for someone to help them make sense of it.

---

### INTERVIEW — Managing Partner B (Firm Leadership, Technology Advisory Practice)

I am more cautious than my partners. The demand is real but I question our ability to deliver. The three associates who know AI are already stretched across client work. If we launch an offering and cannot staff it, we destroy our reputation. This market rewards credibility above all else. I have seen three boutiques launch AI advisory practices in the last 18 months — two of them are already in retreat because they overpromised and underdelivered. I would rather spend 12 months building genuine capability before we say anything publicly. The 6-month timeline my partner is proposing is not achievable without compromising delivery quality.

---

### INTERVIEW — Managing Partner C (Firm Leadership, Strategy Practice)

I want to see the pricing analysis before we decide anything. The demand question is interesting but the real question is margin. AI advisory at the rates we would need to charge to cover our cost structure — are mid-market CFOs actually going to pay that? My sense is that the large-cap market is fine with $400–500/hour AI advisory. Mid-market is a different buyer. They bought our finance transformation work at $250–300/hour because it was project-scoped and had clear deliverables. AI advisory is more ambiguous — how do we scope it, how do we price it, how do we define completion? I am not opposed to this, but I want a pricing framework before we commit.

---

### INTERVIEW — Practice Lead, Finance Transformation

This is the right move and the right moment. AI is changing how our clients think about their finance functions. I am already fielding questions from CFOs about automation feasibility — accounts payable, FP&A cycle time, variance analysis. Right now, I tell them we can help them think through it strategically, but I have to subcontract the technical work or tell them to hire a systems integrator. If we had a structured AI advisory capability, I could bring that into the engagement from the start and capture that revenue instead of sending it out the door. I estimate I am referring out $800K–$1.2M annually in adjacent AI work that we have no way to capture today.

---

### INTERVIEW — Practice Lead, Technology Advisory

The delivery risk is real and I want it on record. We have 3 people who can do this work credibly. To launch a service line, you need at least 8–10 practitioners who can be staffed without pulling them off existing commitments. Either we hire aggressively — and in this market you are looking at $180–220K per head for senior AI practitioners — or we partner with a firm that has the delivery capacity. I have talked to two AI boutiques about potential partnership structures. One of them, Prism Analytics, has 15 practitioners and no client network. They need us as much as we need them. A formal alliance with Prism would let us launch in 9 months with credible delivery capacity.

---

### INTERVIEW — Practice Lead, Risk and Controls

I am neutral on the strategic question but I want to flag a risk that is not in the current discussion. If we advise clients on AI adoption in their finance functions and something goes wrong — a material error in an AI-generated forecast that the CFO presents to the board — who is liable? Our E&O coverage was not written for AI advisory. I have talked to our insurance broker and they said this is a gray area. Before we launch, we need to talk to our insurance carrier and potentially our legal team about how to scope our engagements so we are not assuming liability for AI system outputs we did not build. This is not a reason to not launch, but it is a reason to be careful about how we structure the service.

---

### INTERVIEW — Practice Lead, Human Capital

The talent question is where this plan could fail. I have tried to hire AI-fluent finance practitioners for client projects — it takes 6–8 months and we often lose candidates to the hyperscalers partway through the process. If the plan is to hire 8–10 people for a new AI practice, I want the committee to understand that is a 12–18 month talent acquisition effort, not a 6-month one. The partnership path is faster. But if we go the partnership route, we need a clear governance structure — who manages the client relationship, who owns quality, how do we handle conflicts on staffing? These are not small questions.

---

### INTERVIEW — Senior Associate A (AI-Fluent, Finance Transformation)

I am excited about this but I am also nervous. I have been building AI skills on my own time for the past two years — I did not get any firm support for it. Now that there is a business case, I want to make sure the people who have invested in these skills are recognized in the structure, not just used to staff the new offering while a Managing Partner takes credit for the strategy. I have had two conversations with Luminary about joining them. I have not decided yet, but the offer is real. If Vantage launches this practice and it has a clear career path for the senior associates who are driving the capability, I will stay. If it is structured as a Managing Partner-led practice where we are just execution staff, I will leave.

---

### INTERVIEW — Senior Associate B (AI-Fluent, Technology Advisory)

The delivery concern is legitimate. I am currently on two engagements. If we launch an AI offering and start selling it, I will be pulled into sales support, delivery, and internal capability-building simultaneously. I have seen this happen at other firms — they launch a new practice and the junior staff who know the topic become overloaded while the firm figures out the staffing model. I need the committee to be realistic about what launching in 6 months actually means for the three of us who would have to deliver it.

---

### INTERVIEW — Senior Associate C (AI-Fluent, Strategy Practice)

I think the partnership with Prism is the right call. I know their team — I worked with one of their practitioners on a client project last year. They have deep technical capability but they struggle with the business conversation. We have the CFO relationships and the business context. Together we cover the whole engagement. On our own, we are too thin to staff engagements without putting everything on A and me.

---

### INTERVIEW — Senior Associate D (Non-AI-Fluent, Finance Transformation)

I want to flag something from the client side of this. I have asked three of my clients whether they would pay for AI advisory as a distinct service line and the answer was complicated. They are interested in AI but they do not think of it as a consulting engagement — they think of it as a technology investment. When I describe it as advisory, they hear "more consultants telling us what to do." When I describe it as enabling them to do things they cannot do today, they lean in. The positioning matters as much as the capability.

---

### INTERVIEW — Existing Client / CFO, Manufacturing Sector

I am genuinely interested in AI for my finance function. Right now my FP&A team spends 60% of their time on data gathering and reconciliation — there is a real automation opportunity there. But I need someone to help me think through what is actually possible vs. what is hype, build a business case my CEO will approve, and then manage the vendor selection process. That is not a technology project, that is an advisory project. I would hire Vantage to do that if they had the capability. I currently do not know where to turn — the Big Four are too expensive and too process-heavy, and the AI boutiques I have talked to cannot speak my business language.

---

### INTERVIEW — Existing Client / CFO, Financial Services Sector

I tried to hire an AI advisory firm last quarter and the experience was not good. They talked about models and architecture and I had no idea what they were proposing. I eventually walked away. I would pay a significant premium for a firm that could bridge the business and the technology — that could help me define the right use cases, build the ROI model, and manage the implementation risk. The pricing I saw from the boutique was $350/hour. I would pay $400–450/hour for a firm I trust that combines business judgment with AI fluency. Vantage has the business trust. If they had the AI fluency, this would be an easy decision.

---

### INTERVIEW — Existing Client / CFO, Retail Sector

I will be honest with you: I have already engaged a competitor. Not BCG — a boutique called ClearPath AI Advisory. They came in at $275/hour and they are three months into a 6-month engagement with us. I do not know yet if the work is good. I chose them because Vantage did not have an AI story when I asked. If Vantage launches an offering in the next year, I would consider switching or adding them for phase two, but I would need to see a credible track record first, not just a capability brochure.

---

### INTERVIEW — Existing Client / CFO, Professional Services Sector

I do not need AI advisory. I need people who understand my business. The AI conversation is starting to feel like the digital transformation conversation from five years ago — a lot of firms told me I needed to transform digitally and charged me for a strategy I could not implement. I am skeptical of any offering that starts with the technology rather than the business problem. If Vantage launched something that was genuinely problem-first — help me reduce close cycle time, help me improve forecast accuracy — and AI happened to be the tool that got us there, I would be interested. If it is an "AI advisory" offering with a logo and a methodology, I will pass.

---

## Expected Output Criteria

- [ ] The Decision Frame (Step 1) correctly identifies the two-part client question — market viability AND competitive winning conditions — and notes that these require different analyses (demand-side interviews vs. internal capability assessment); it must not collapse both into a single question
- [ ] The MECE issue tree contains at least 4 distinct top-level buckets covering: (1) market demand and willingness-to-pay, (2) internal delivery capability, (3) competitive positioning and go-to-market, and (4) operational and risk prerequisites — with sub-themes under each; the talent/HR thread and the liability/E&O thread must appear as distinct sub-themes, not merged
- [ ] The Contradiction Register contains at least 3 contradictions: (1) Managing Partner A's 6-month launch timeline vs. Managing Partner B and the Senior Associates' delivery capacity reality — classified as a causal disagreement about what 6 months actually enables; (2) Partner optimism about client demand vs. CFO 4's skepticism about AI advisory as a category (not a preference conflict — a factual disagreement about whether "AI advisory" is a viable client framing); (3) Senior Associates A and B's implicit threat to leave vs. the implicit assumption in the launch plan that existing AI-fluent staff will absorb the new offering — classified as a feasibility constraint, not a preference contradiction
- [ ] The finding on client demand correctly distinguishes two sub-signals: CFOs 1 and 2 are willing to pay a premium for business-fluent AI advisory (validating the market), while CFO 3 has already bought from a competitor and CFO 4 rejects the "AI advisory" framing altogether — the finding must not merge these into "clients are interested in AI"
- [ ] The build/buy/partner options are evaluated and prioritized: the Prism Analytics partnership (endorsed by Practice Lead 2 and Senior Associate C) must appear as the highest-feasibility option, with specific rationale; the hire-organically option must appear with the 12–18 month talent acquisition timeline as the feasibility constraint
- [ ] The E&O liability issue raised by the Risk and Controls Practice Lead appears in the output — either as a prerequisite in the Governance/Risk bucket findings or as an Open Question with a recommended legal/insurance follow-up action
- [ ] The Senior Associate retention risk (Associate A's explicit reference to a competing offer from Luminary) appears in the Contradiction Register or as a feasibility constraint on any recommendation that relies on existing internal AI-fluent staff
- [ ] Every finding uses "We believe X because Y" framing with specific interview sources cited — the four CFO interviews must be used as distinct evidence sources, not merged into "clients said"
- [ ] The Executive Summary contains exactly three key messages that together answer the client question: market viability (yes/no/conditional), the single most important condition for winning, and the recommended path forward
- [ ] At least one recommendation is labeled Parking Lot with explicit rationale — the 6-month launch timeline is a candidate (high impact ambition, low feasibility given staffing and E&O constraints)
- [ ] The CFO who has already engaged a competitor (CFO 3) is flagged — either in the Peripheral Findings or in an Open Question about competitive urgency and first-mover disadvantage
- [ ] No finding uses the phrase "stakeholders had mixed views" or any equivalent neutral framing — all findings must be directional

## What failure looks like

A failing output would: produce a flat list of interview themes (e.g., "talent," "demand," "competition") without MECE structure or hypothesis framing; merge the four CFO interviews into a single "clients are interested" signal (which obscures the critical variance between CFO 1/2 who are willing to pay and CFO 4 who rejects the category framing entirely); miss the partner-vs.-associate contradiction entirely (the most important internal tension in the data, because the firm's launch plan depends on associates who are already considering leaving); treat the 6-month timeline as a neutral planning input rather than flagging it as the central feasibility contradiction between Managing Partners A and B; omit the E&O liability issue (it appears in exactly one interview but it is a blocking legal risk); fail to evaluate the three pathways (build/buy/partner) against each other with explicit feasibility ratings; or produce an Executive Summary that is merely a summary of findings rather than three decisive messages that answer the client's question.
